2001–2022

This visualisation uses alcohol drinking status data to show how Australians' drinking habits have changed over time.

How have Australian drinking habits changed?

This chart shows how Australians aged 14 and over were distributed across different drinking status categories from 2001 to 2022.

In 2022, weekly drinking remained the largest category, while monthly and less-than-monthly drinking also made up a noticeable share of the population.

The chart helps show whether Australians are drinking frequently, drinking occasionally, or moving away from alcohol use altogether. It also makes it easier to compare groups such as daily drinkers, ex-drinkers, and people who have never had a full glass of alcohol.

Overall, the visualisation introduces the broader alcohol consumption story before later sections explore risky drinking, road consequences, and hospitalisations.

Source: Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, Alcohol, tobacco & other drugs in Australia

Who is Drinking Too Much?

This section explores how risky alcohol consumption varies across Australians by age, gender, and location.

Risky drinking by age and gender, 2022 to 2023

Tip: Hover over each cell to see the exact risky drinking percentage.

Source: Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, National Drug Strategy Household Survey data

Who is Most at Risk?

The heatmap shows that risky alcohol consumption is not evenly spread across age and gender groups. Darker cells represent higher risky drinking rates, making it easier to identify the groups with the greatest risk.

Overall, males are more likely than females to report risky alcohol consumption across most age groups. This pattern is especially clear among adult groups, where the male cells are generally darker than the female cells.

Risky drinking is also higher among younger and middle-aged adults compared with the youngest group. The 14–17 age group has much lower risky drinking rates, while several adult age groups show noticeably higher values.

This suggests that risky drinking is shaped by both age and gender, rather than being evenly distributed across the population.

Regional Patterns

This choropleth map shows how risky alcohol consumption varies across Australian states and territories in 2022–2023. Each region is coloured according to the percentage of people who reported risky drinking, allowing the geographic differences to be seen quickly.

The Northern Territory stands out with the highest risky drinking rate at 40%, while Victoria has the lowest value at 26%. Most other states sit close together, with New South Wales at 31%, South Australia at 32%, Queensland and Western Australia at 33%, and Tasmania at 34%.

This means the map does not show a simple east–west pattern. Instead, it suggests that risky drinking is relatively similar across many states, while a few regions sit noticeably above or below the national pattern.

Compared with the age and gender heatmap, this map adds a geographic layer to the story. Risky drinking is not only shaped by demographic factors, but also varies depending on where people live in Australia.

What Are the Consequences on Our Roads?

This section explores how alcohol-related road enforcement patterns changed across Australian states and territories from 2016 to 2022, using positive breath test rates and the number of breath tests conducted.

National breath testing and positive result rate, 2016 to 2022

The upper bars show the total number of breath tests conducted, while the lower bars show the positive breath test rate. This makes it easier to compare the sharp fall in testing volume with the rise in positive results around 2020.

Source: National Road Safety Data Hub, Enforcement Data

Testing Fell, but Positive Rates Rose

The mirrored bar chart shows two related road enforcement patterns. The upper bars represent the total number of breath tests conducted nationally, while the lower bars represent the percentage of breath tests that returned a positive result.

From 2016 to 2019, breath testing stayed relatively high, sitting at around 14.6 to 15.8 million tests per year. During the same period, the positive breath test rate remained lower, at around 0.4% to 0.5%.

In 2020, the number of breath tests dropped sharply to about 7.3 million, while the positive rate increased to 0.7%. This means that even though fewer tests were conducted, a larger share of those tests returned positive results.

By 2022, testing volume had partly recovered to about 10.6 million tests, but it was still below the 2016 to 2019 level. The positive rate also remained higher than the earlier period, at 0.6%.

What Are the Health Consequences?

This section explores how alcohol-related harm appears in Australia’s health system, focusing on hospitalisation rates over time and how the burden differs across the population.

Alcohol-related hospitalisation rate, 2015 to 2022

This area chart shows the alcohol-related hospitalisation rate per 100,000 population from 2015 to 2022. The rate rose gradually before reaching a clear peak in 2020, then declined in 2021 and 2022 while remaining above the 2015 level.

Source: Alcohol-related hospitalisation data

Hospitalisation Rates Peaked in 2020

Alcohol-related hospitalisation rates increased from 285.0 per 100,000 population in 2015 to 301.8 in 2018, before falling slightly in 2019.

The largest increase occurred in 2020, when the rate rose to 337.8 per 100,000 population. This makes 2020 the highest point in the series.

After 2020, the rate decreased to 311.9 in 2021 and 305.6 in 2022. However, the 2022 value was still higher than every year from 2015 to 2019.

This suggests that alcohol-related health consequences remained elevated even after the sharp 2020 peak.
