Exploring how alcohol drinking habits among Australians aged 14 and over have changed from 2001 to 2022.

2001–2022

This visualisation uses alcohol drinking status data to show how Australians' drinking habits have changed over time.

How have Australian drinking habits changed?

This line graph compares different drinking status groups among Australians aged 14 and over from 2001 to 2022.

Each line represents a different drinking status category, such as daily drinking, weekly drinking, monthly drinking, ex-drinkers, and people who have never had a full glass of alcohol.

The chart helps show whether Australians are drinking more frequently, less frequently, or shifting into different drinking behaviour groups over time.

Data values are shown as percentages.

Source: Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, Alcohol, tobacco & other drugs in Australia

Who is Most at Risk?

The heatmap shows that risky alcohol consumption is not evenly spread across age and gender groups. Darker cells represent higher risky drinking rates, making it easier to identify the groups with the greatest risk.

Overall, males are more likely than females to report risky alcohol consumption across most age groups. This pattern is especially clear among adult groups, where the male cells are generally darker than the female cells.

Risky drinking is also higher among younger and middle-aged adults compared with the youngest group. The 14–17 age group has much lower risky drinking rates, while several adult age groups show noticeably higher values.

This suggests that risky drinking is shaped by both age and gender, rather than being evenly distributed across the population.

Regional Patterns

This choropleth map shows how risky alcohol consumption varies across Australian states and territories in 2022–2023. Each region is coloured according to the percentage of people who reported risky drinking, allowing the geographic differences to be seen quickly.

The Northern Territory stands out with the highest risky drinking rate at 40%, while Victoria has the lowest value at 26%. Most other states sit close together, with New South Wales at 31%, South Australia at 32%, Queensland and Western Australia at 33%, and Tasmania at 34%.

This means the map does not show a simple east–west pattern. Instead, it suggests that risky drinking is relatively similar across many states, while a few regions sit noticeably above or below the national pattern.

Compared with the age and gender heatmap, this map adds a geographic layer to the story. Risky drinking is not only shaped by demographic factors, but also varies depending on where people live in Australia.
